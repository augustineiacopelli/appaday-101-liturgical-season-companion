The celebrations of this liturgical year, with the vestment color actually worn on each. Movable dates follow Easter; fixed dates follow the calendar. A celebration outranked by the day it lands on is marked not kept, and the color shown is the one worn in its place. That is a separate matter from a precept being lifted: when the Assumption, All Saints, or Mary, Mother of God falls on a Saturday or a Monday, the United States drops the obligation but still keeps the solemnity in white. Memorials and optional memorials are included: an optional memorial is marked may be kept, since the priest chooses, and an obligatory memorial falling in Lent or late Advent drops to commemoration only. Easter can fall no earlier than 22 March and no later than 25 April, a swing of 35 days that every movable date in the year inherits. Nothing here is fetched. The whole calendar is arithmetic your device runs in a few microseconds.

Three celebrations move depending on where you are. These are set for the United States by default, where the bishops have transferred all three to Sunday. Your choices are remembered on this device.

The Epiphany of the Lord
Traditionally 6 January. In the United States and much of the world it is kept on the Sunday between 2 and 8 January. This also sets the Baptism of the Lord, which closes Christmas.
The Ascension of the Lord
Forty days after Easter falls on a Thursday. Most provinces in the United States, including Kansas, transfer it to the Seventh Sunday of Easter.
The Most Holy Body and Blood of Christ
Corpus Christi. Traditionally the Thursday after Trinity Sunday, transferred to the following Sunday in the United States.

This companion follows the General Roman Calendar with the United States proper calendar layered on top. It shows seasons, ranks, colors, and cycles down through the optional memorials. It is not an ordo: it does not carry local patrons, diocesan propers, votive Masses, or the transfers a particular year may require, and it makes no choice among the optional memorials that share a date. Your parish or diocesan ordo governs.
